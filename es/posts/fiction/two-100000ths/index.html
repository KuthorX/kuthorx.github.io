Challenge to the Reader

There will never be only a slow lament in this world, nor an endless night. Fans of orthodox mystery fiction (including the author) should note that this is not a reader challenge in the classical Ellery Queen style, nor in the modern Honkaku style of Shimada Sōji. As the Reader’s Note says, this is not an orthodox mystery novel. Fans of orthodox mysteries (yes, including the author) love to dissect the puzzle before the challenge, then feel satisfaction, doubt, surprise, or wonder when opening the solution. However unconventional or perceptive the deductions may be, the author notices that all of this presupposes one thing: the question has an answer. If there is no answer, how can there be a challenge to the reader? If the truth is sad, infuriating, frightening, or maddening, should it be hidden? If the truth admits many interpretations—like a thousand Hamlets in a thousand eyes, and like every work ceasing to belong to its author the moment it is published—is the truth itself important? From the perspective of a human, the author’s answer is: Whatever. The author is human, a human just like a robot implanted with K-1647—capable of self-learning, with no central neural network connected to me, possessing independent consciousness and unpredictable behavior (BRAIN-IN-A-VATs; you have the right to remain silent)—and at this moment I do not care about these questions. So what does the author want to challenge readers with? As stated at the beginning, this is a work humanity is destined never to complete. Robots (and today’s AI) are also destined never to complete it. Then: who can complete it? Readers are welcome to write with feedback.

Prologue: The First Lament

A teacher should not merely teach books; his duty is to teach people how to live. A student should not merely read books; his duty is to learn the way of life. — Tao Xingzhi 2000, date unknown Moyan High School Library On summer nights at Moyan High, toads called without end. Huang Yeling had taught there for years and was used to it. She taught senior-year Chinese and also ran the library, where she sat reading The Count of Monte Cristo. Few students came at night. There were more than ten copies of most books, and the entrance was beside the desk, so she gradually lost herself in the story. Before she finished Chapter Two, a student entered. Without greeting her, the student went to the shelves. She felt annoyed, then decided the student probably thought she was studying and did not want to interrupt. The student soon brought several books. “Teacher, I want to borrow these.” The low stack looked odd. Worse, the student wore gloves. “All right.” She stood. The top book fell with a slap, and the toads abruptly fell silent. “Sorry, I will pick it up.” The student vaulted the counter just as she bent down. “Here is your book.” She straightened and raised it to the student. The student stuffed a towel into her mouth and twisted her arms behind her. She tried to call for help, but the towel swallowed her voice. The calm student had become icy. “The Count of Monte Cristo? You have taste… It suits me. I came for revenge.” The student drew a shining dagger. Though the library had few fluorescent lights, its reflection looked like a demon’s gaze. The blade entered her abdomen. She screamed again; the sound was cruelly absorbed. The student released her and vanished down the corridor. Knowing rescue was impossible, she struggled to expose the killer and wrote the student’s name on page eleven of The Count of Monte Cristo. Outside, a sparrow shrieked for no reason. Together with the toads beginning again, it played the prelude to five years of lament.

Part One: The Lament Five Years Later

I believe motive and characterization complement each other. When someone is driven into a dead end, that mental state forms the motive for crime… Add sociality, and the possibilities of the mystery novel become broad. — Seicho Matsumoto

Chapter One

2005, date unknown 9:05 a.m. Posen Wall's home Posen Wall had just climbed out of bed. It was Sunday; he did not have to work. The alarm said five past nine. Why get up so early? He lay down again. The doorbell made him jump. “Hello, Speedy Express! Mr. Wall? A delivery for you. Please sign.” Speedy Express? Too speedy—I was sleeping! After complaining inwardly, Posen found himself wide awake and opened the door. The energetic young courier confirmed his identity and said George Anos had paid the urgent shipping fee; only a signature was needed. George Anos? They had chatted happily a few days earlier. Why not call or email? Why an urgent delivery? Posen signed. After the courier left, he opened the thin envelope. It contained one letter: Dear Posen, My hands tremble as I write. This is a decision five years in the making. Do you remember the book? The Count of Monte Cristo, the book in which Teacher Huang Yeling wrote her death message. Page 11 was torn out because it bore someone’s name… Yes, I hid the evidence, preventing police from finding the killer. Forgive me: I did not want that person in the dock and prison. I was weak and could not tell right from wrong. Now I will face reality and meet that person this afternoon. Do not worry about me or come over. That person still does not dare harm me. Come around 7:30 tonight. That person will come too. I know they may kill me tonight, but please do not interfere. That is all. I have things to do. See you tonight. Yours, still wandering between WHO, George Anos Posen pondered. Anos had torn it out? The killer would do anything for that page, but Anos must have hidden it. Was he blackmailing the killer? No; Posen knew him. Why go tonight—to let the killer enjoy an evening, then surrender together? Too naïve. Should he call police? Would they believe the letter? The old case was far away, and “may kill me” was only a possibility. There was not enough evidence for action. Posen sighed. Though foreboding spread through him, he forced himself calm. He could only reconstruct the five-year-old murder in his mind.

Chapter Two

2005, date unknown 7:00 p.m. Posen Wall's home Anos’s villa address was on the back of the letter. Was this his first and last visit? Posen shook his head. Stay optimistic. A year earlier Anos had written that, as a teacher at Moyan High, he might spend summer near Posen’s apartment. They began chatting and calling every day, and Anos often visited. Now Posen could finally visit him, carrying this feeling… Posen had no car and took a taxi. After high school everyone scattered. Anos came from a good family and attended university; Posen entered society and now worked for a clothing company, saving money. “Lavega Lane,” Posen told the driver.

The taxi moved slowly along the concrete road. Posen reread the letter. “Wandering between WHO”—did it mean the letter H, between Heaven and Hell? If it expressed conflict, why be so obscure? Or did WHO mean “who,” or the World Health Organization? Impossible. The driver chattered about Lavega’s scenery, prices, and amusement parks, describing the city as paradise. Posen sneered. He disliked city images: flat-roofed houses, arrogant towers, fake middle-class estates, affected greenery—worse than light pollution. The driver continued; Posen endured. They were almost there. The taxi entered Lavega Lane. The driver finally fell silent, apparently appreciating the view. “Wow, how elegant!” he said. Anos had chosen well. The city’s noise vanished. Wild grass hugged the road, trees stood on both sides, and Lavega Lake glimmered in the distance. At last Posen saw scenery that improved his mood. The villa emerged. Blue and yellow blended with the woods: a pale-blue door set casually in the right wall, yellow-white walls, two large windows flanking the door, and two close-set windows for other rooms. “This is heaven too,” the driver said before leaving. Perhaps hell as well. Posen did not say it.

Chapter Three

2005-07-11 7:48 p.m. Outside George Anos's villa The taxi stopped a few dozen meters away. The road ran straight, turned right, and reached the door; around the villa it formed a loop. Posen climbed brown wooden steps that creaked. There were few steps, so the screams did not last long. He rang. The door opened. A man in his fifties stood there. Apart from gray hair and wrinkles, he looked fit enough for WWE. “Hello, I am Posen Wall, Anos’s friend.” The old man smiled kindly. “Master Anos often mentions you. I am Chen Pingguo, the butler. Come in.” Posen closed the door and followed him, looking around. The corridor lights were on. Anos liked geometry: its walls were covered in cream tiles printed with circles, equilateral triangles, and even circles drawn with straight lines. “No wonder—he is a math teacher.” They turned right, then left, and reached the parlor. Two familiar faces appeared: John Clisen, the mathematics representative, and Jane Rollman, the art representative, from senior year. “I thought I was the only guest,” Posen joked. “Of course not.” John waved. With sandy hair from his British mother, black eyes and a strong build from his Chinese father, he grinned at Posen. “Do you have nothing to say about our great talent?” He winked at Jane, who smiled shyly. Posen smiled too. Jane had been the boys’ dream from first to third year: long brown hair, blue eyes, excellent grades, and depth. Even Posen had liked her. She remained unmarried and became a successful professional painter. John studied economics and became a property developer. He told them prices were rising and houses harder to sell, though speculation was easy. “I could lend you some savings—I might buy from you.” “Good idea; I will give you ten percent off!” Everyone laughed. As the maid served fruit salad, Chinese representative Wang Xinchang arrived. He seemed rough but wrote brilliantly and now edited Lavega’s newspaper. Everyone joked that failing to start a publishing company wasted his talent. Model Margo Binsongji enlivened the room. She was tall and elegant; boys joked she was Marilyn Monroe. Xinchang would have Posen’s manager tailor clothes, John sell her a house, Jane paint her, and Xinchang publish it: “Our five immortals each show their skill!” For a moment Posen was back in a tense, happy senior year: after school they abandoned homework, rigid teachers, and absurd rules, heading toward sunset—each to their own home. “Why has the host not appeared?” John asked. Only then did everyone notice George was absent. “Sorry,” the butler said. “Perhaps not everyone has arrived. I remember another gentleman—Hippomenes Mosifa.” “Putting on airs. Let us call him. Two beauties are waiting!” Xinchang stood laughing. “I will come too.” Posen smiled, but knew the important question was whether Anos was safe. “We need evidence that they are beauties! I will come too.” Jane stood. “Master Anos says three or four people in the corridor are enough or it gets crowded—what was it? ‘Among three people, one is my teacher,’ haha.” Those seated laughed and shook their heads. The old man loved quotations. “Master Anos does not like being disturbed while writing, so he uses the corridor light to tell me whether he has time.” The butler looked puzzled. “But… he has not heard us?” “Do you have a spare key?” Posen began to worry. “Yes, in my pocket.” The butler searched his pocket. “George! Are you awake? If you are busy, tell us—we can wait!” Jane called. There was still no reply. The butler inserted the key and turned it. “Locked from inside? Strange. Master Anos never locks it…” “Break the door down!” Posen blurted. “Yes. What if something happened…” Jane grew uneasy. “George, we are going to break the door!” Though Xinchang represented Chinese class, he never minced words. He turned to the butler and Posen. “The three of us together.” They nodded. “One, two, three!” The door barely moved. “One, two, three!” With a bang, the door burst open and the three stumbled into darkness. Worse, at the instant the door opened, a window somewhere in the room seemed to shatter. Glass crackled through the room and stabbed Posen’s ears. A duller sound followed; later he identified it as a chair falling onto the cobbled path. “Turn on the light!” Posen shouted. The butler hurried to the switch on the left wall. The room lit up. When Posen’s eyes adjusted, he saw the shattered French window behind and to the right of a table. Wol felt dizzy. Holding his breath, he walked deeper into the room. Anos gradually came into view: a dagger had been driven into his abdomen; his tilted head rested against the wall between two windows; his hands hung naturally, his legs were slightly apart, and his eyes stared wide open. Over Rollman’s scream, Wol saw a book lying by Anos’s right hand. He stepped closer and found that, just like The Count of Monte Cristo five years ago, pages 11 and 12 had vanished without a trace… Meanwhile, a few birds and crickets in the bushes slowly began to sing. To Wol, it was the lament that had been playing for five years.

Chapter Five

July 11, 2005 8:19 p.m. George Anos’s bedroom “Butler Chen, take Jane outside. We need to preserve the scene. Call the police at once.” The butler nodded shakily and dragged the nearly rigid Jane out. Wol tried to calm himself, opened the door, and backed into the corridor. He heard another doorbell… “The killer has escaped,” he muttered. “We can’t catch him…” He regretted not intervening. George, how could you be so naïve? Did you really think the killer would believe you? That he would let this go? Even if we find him in the end, your death was not worth this! “I’ll go after him—don’t worry about me.” Xin Chang forced a smile, but Wol could still see the shock on his face. “Protect the scene here. I’ll chase the killer!” He jumped through the floor-to-ceiling window at the end of the corridor. Wol hurried back into the room. After making sure no one was nearby, he went straight to the body. Wol put on gloves, just in case—so he really would need them after all, he thought, shaking his head—and summoned the courage to examine Anos’s body. Crickets chirped continuously in the distance, with an owl calling from time to time. Wol carefully moved to the right side of the body and gently opened the book… This was not The Count of Monte Cristo but a selection of Maupassant’s stories. Strangely, page eleven had been torn out again. Why had the killer done this? Why not kill George at once? Was it to force him to reveal where the missing pages were? If so, why make George write something in the book? Saying it aloud would have been faster than writing it down. And had George actually written anything? If so, when, and what? If he had not, why tear the page out? Did the Chinese characters on page eleven contain the killer’s name? No—that combination of characters could form too many names; the killer would have no reason to fear the police finding him that way… There were too many questions at the scene to make sense of. Wol could only wait for the police to examine the evidence. A little discouraged, he turned the book back to the missing-page section, left the room, and leaned against the corridor wall to think. After a minute, Xin Chang climbed in through the corridor’s floor-to-ceiling window and shook his head. “He’s gone—I was too slow to react… It looks like a garden outside, though something feels strange.” “It wasn’t your fault…” Wol waved. “Go back to the sitting room. Tell everyone what happened and reassure them.” The butler came over. His eyes were still dull. “Mr. Wol, I’ve called the police. They say they’ll arrive in about ten minutes. The others are in the sitting room.” “We’ll keep watch here. All we can do is wait for the police.” Wol looked at the night sky and thought of the letter Anos had sent him… “Oh, and Mr. Mosesfa has already arrived. He came just as I called the police.” Butler Chen spoke with a puzzled look.

The laughter in the sitting room had long since been drowned out by the news of Anos’s death. Silence had taken its place. Wol walked up to the solemn-looking Mosesfa. “Hippo, come outside for a moment.”

“Me?” Mosesfa stood at the front door, angry and confused. “Listen, do you think it was me?” “No.” Wol waved hurriedly. “I only want to confirm what you were doing when it happened. I don’t want the others to feel uncomfortable; I need to put everyone at ease.” “I came by taxi,” Mosesfa said with a shrug. “You know how bad traffic is in La Vega. This driver was especially bad at conversation, so I was bored to death the whole way. I planned to stay for five minutes, but before even one minute had passed I heard a sound and went to knock on the door…” “Why were you planning to stay five minutes?” “Huh? Wasn’t that what George asked? Didn’t he tell you? He told me to stay five minutes after arriving…” “Hmm… Did you notice anything strange on the way here?” “No.” Wol suddenly realized that all six of them had come by taxi—something he had learned during their cheerful conversation earlier. “I thought we had a clue…” Wol sighed. “Hey, hey, how can you interrogate me in front of your friends? Honestly, you scared me…” Mosesfa was still exactly as he had been in their senior year. He and Wol had been an odd pair back then, idling around school all day and barely different from delinquents—in Wol’s words, “delinquents in artistic clothing.” Wol could not help launching into a long chat with him. Mosesfa had no fixed job and was currently a newspaper deliveryman; he had tried almost every kind of work. The pay was low, but Mosesfa believed it was unreasonable to work monotonously just to receive money. People were not machines, after all. The two returned to the sitting room, laughing and talking. Seeing them, the others said nothing more. The atmosphere was no longer so tense.

Chapter Six

July 11, 2005 8:34 p.m. George Anos’s villa, sitting room Insects and birds continued calling as the harsh sirens drew closer. Wol felt his head swell. The doorbell rang again, and several police officers entered the sitting room. “Sorry to disturb you,” said the polite leader. “This is the house where the murder occurred?” “Yes, sir. My name is Chen Pingguo, the butler. I will take you to Young Master Anos’s bedroom.” The leader nodded and said that everyone present would be searched. “Wait,” Wol stood. “I am coming too,” though he thought the chance was slim. “Hmm?” The leader seemed to be a detective inspector. “You are?” “Posen Wol, the victim’s friend,” Wol said, summoning his courage. “All right,” the inspector said after a moment. “I am Massol Riley, the inspector responsible for this area.” The inspector was Massol Riley! Wol had not seen him since a small incident on a train four years earlier (unrelated to this case). Wol had learned many investigative techniques in his spare time, but never found time to seek out the “old man.” Meeting him by chance now, he quietly counted himself lucky. Wol described the case, and Riley understood the outline. “A five-year-old case? When I return to headquarters I will call the Moyan District police and have them fax over the old files. By the way, are you still a junior clerk at a clothing company? What a waste of talent!” Wol said nothing. It was the twenty-first century, he thought. Detectives did boring work; he was interested only in cold and strange cases, and there were not many of those in this world. He might as well read mysteries at the clothing company. He had no interest in becoming a policeman; he disliked being bound by rules. “Do whatever you like.” Riley seemed to know what Wol was thinking and shrugged. “As long as you do nothing bad. Investigate with those gloves on.” He patted Wol’s shoulder. “I will not interfere. I have already told the detectives to inform you of anything they find; you may question them too. We are old classmates, after all. I have to go to the station; your classmates will give statements with us. Here is my number. See you later.” Riley handed Wol a card and left.

The searches naturally revealed nothing. Wol listened to Mosesfa’s quiet complaints and watched him leave. Wol entered Anos’s bedroom. Wol went to Zhang Yan. “What is the estimated time of death?” Zhang had been Wol’s deskmate. She had told him then that she wanted to become a doctor; he never expected her to become a forensic pathologist. “A little over half an hour ago. My estimate should be right, but the exact time must wait for the autopsy.” Zhang frowned. Wol did not know the two detectives on the other side of the room, Lu Ranzhi and Lu Hang. He forced himself to ask Ranzhi, “Any discoveries?” “Why should I tell you? Can’t you look for yourself?” Ranzhi replied irritably. “Ranzhi, be polite,” Zhang said loudly. “Inspector Riley said he would not interfere. Does that mean we cannot share information?” She turned to Wol. “Sorry, he is not always like this.” Wol felt embarrassed. He was an outsider and had no proper reason to join the investigation, but he could not let this chance go. “Lu Ranzhi, I truly want to solve this case. The dead man was a close friend—not quite like a brother, but we got along very well. I knew this morning that he was going to die, but I could not stop it. Now I cannot even help. If you were me, would you not want to help too?” Silence followed. “People cannot help simply because they want to,” Wol noticed the change in Ranzhi’s tone. “Back then I also—never mind. I have work to do… Riley often talked about you at our reunions. I take back what I said.” “What did you say you found?” “We have only examined part of the scene. The only certainty is that there are no signs of a struggle. We need several more hours for a systematic conclusion. Rest for now.” Ranzhi spoke in a completely different tone. “All right. Thank you.” Wol felt grief mixed with the excitement of solving a case, and his mind could no longer bear it. He decided to rest for a few hours. Before leaving, Posen whispered to Ranzhi, “Thank you.” Lu Ranzhi nodded.

Wol returned to the sitting room and found it empty. “The inspector took everyone to the station to give statements. Before leaving, he told you to stay here and said you need not worry about work tomorrow; he would explain things to your manager. Sleep in my bedroom tonight—we have a spare straw mat.” “Thank you.” Wol thanked the butler. Ranzhi had started to say something earlier but stopped halfway. Had he once faced something like what I did? No, no—my attention should be on this case. Through the floor-to-ceiling window beside the entrance, Wol saw several guards already protecting the villa.

Part Two: Beneath the Lament

Death is the original state; survival is like a mechanical failure. Life itself is a bug. —— Hiroshi Mori

Chapter Seven

July 12, 2005 4:36 a.m. George Anos’s villa, butler’s bedroom Wol woke groggily in the dark. The luminous hands of his watch pointed to 4:37. He got up and walked to Anos’s bedroom. “I’m here.” Wol yawned and saw that the body had been removed. “How is the investigation going?” “Almost done,” Zhang Yan said. “Let me tell you what we know…” Wol organised the facts:

  1. The basic layout of the room has been drawn (see Figures One and Two).
  2. The victim fell between the two windows in the left wall. He died from blood loss after a dagger pierced his abdomen, about one minute later; the time of death was between seven and eight.
  3. A selection of Maupassant’s stories lay near the victim’s right hand. Pages 11 and 12 had been torn out; no impressions were found on page 13, and the rest was intact.
  4. Many shards of glass were found below the room’s only floor-to-ceiling window. Among them was a moderately heavy chair whose height could be adjusted freely. “You should investigate yourself. You might find a new clue,” Zhang suggested. Anos’s room had a relaxed air that even the shadow of death had not erased. The writing desk held many printed articles, samples of well-known magazines that had accepted his work, and a thick stack of drafts. The drafts looked like street graffiti, their handwriting too messy to reveal anything. The research desk beside it was piled with books. Open pages carried Anos’s notes, including a summary of current education. The language was extreme but not unreasonable: “…Where exactly is the focus of our education? On valuing results again and again, or on making students unable to do anything except take exams? In my view, the current system has too many loopholes and needs to be repaired slowly. I only fear that before it is repaired, we will be drowned by countless test papers with nowhere to escape…” This was Anos’s marginal note in Lao She’s To Pass Exams Without Dying Is Divine. Now was not the time to read it. Plain clothes hung neatly in the wardrobe. Wol remembered that the villa had only one floor and no basement. The clothes had probably already been searched; there seemed to be no clue there. The top shelf of the wine rack held famous wines; the bottom held ordinary local liquor such as baijiu. The pillow and quilt on the bed were perfectly arranged. The bedside shelf faced the bed and held bedtime reading: Meditations, The Book of Wisdom, A Little World History Every Day, and so on. The shelf opposite the research desk held books meant to be savoured: Strategies of the Warring States, 2004 Current Affairs Reviews, Crazy Mathematics, and many classics. Wol called them that because each book had a small notebook beside it; Anos had written all his notes there. “You still read so seriously after graduation!” Wol exclaimed. Then he found three notebooks beside a book on the bottom shelf. “What is this? There is no book beside them.” He pulled out the leftmost notebook and found Anos’s diary, beginning on June 28, 2005. Anos had written many fragments and moods in it, but how could anyone finish three diaries? Wol meant to put it back, then hesitated. Could it be a clue? He patiently read on.

Chapter Eight

(George Anos’s diary) June 28, 2005 I have finally moved into the villa! The renovation is nice. Pingguo was worried about me and insisted on looking after me himself. What could I do? He has cared for me since I was born. To spare him as much work as possible, I brought along a capable maid—Li Xue. ……… (Anos’s thoughts on Lao She’s work To Pass Exams Without Dying Is Divine*; they seem incomplete.)* Indecision is not a quality a person should have. One must learn to handle matters decisively… “When you cannot decide, trouble follows” says exactly this. July 2, 2005 Tomorrow I am visiting Posen’s apartment. I am happy, and wonder how that fellow who loves mysteries but became a junior clerk is doing… Thinking of senior year brings that case to mind—it is time to decide… ……… (A complete discussion of O. Henry’s stories The Roads We Take and The Gift of the Magi*.)* The personality formed in secondary school is the foundation of a person’s later life and does not change easily. Some people’s personalities are obvious at a glance, but most require time to understand. Sometimes even years apart—or frequent contact—are not enough to notice how the people around us have changed. This proves that a person’s basic personality is established during a certain period, and that period is high school. July 9, 2005 After a week of consideration, I have decided: I cannot let the killer go free, even if that person is important to me! But can I really make this decision? Perhaps I have made things too complicated. A crime is a crime… By the way, when I visited Posen’s apartment a few days ago, I felt that he was wasting his talent! I cannot claim to know him well, but I can say for certain that his deductive ability is no worse than an ordinary person’s!… (Many words, though not too many, mainly praising “Posen Wol’s outstanding logical skills.” Wol himself felt embarrassed; Charlie believed that this flood of praise was why Wol later became somewhat conceited.) ……… (Anos shifted to Chekhov’s short stories The Chameleon and The Death of a Civil Servant*. Wol breathed a sigh of relief. This discussion was complete too.)* Some people speak their minds; most hide their true selves. This is a person’s instinct for self-protection, not hypocrisy, because everyone has vanity and wariness. July 10, 2005 I will meet that person tomorrow afternoon. I have already called, but did not say why, so at least that person will not strike in the afternoon… I wrote Posen a letter telling him what I intend to do. I do not want to reveal the killer’s name, or even the gender, but I still unconsciously wrote down a clue… Whatever happens, I want to bring this to an end with my own hands, even if it means dying… I told Posen not to worry, but I do not know whether he will impulsively break into the villa. If he discovers this diary, it may become a clue… ……… (Anos then awkwardly discussed an essay by Lu Xun. “Awkward” because the prose was clearly inferior to the previous three entries. Wol frowned and continued.) When someone suddenly becomes strange, completely unlike what you usually believe them to be, you may feel fear, unfamiliarity, even disgust. Put those negative feelings aside. Try to understand them and give them warmth—for at this moment, the person before you is less guarded against you than against anyone else. (End of diary.) July 12, 2005 4:45 a.m. George Anos’s villa, Anos’s bedroom Wol closed the diary with mixed feelings, unaware that Zhang Yan was standing beside him. “How is it?” Zhang shook her hand. “Not good. This diary is too strange. I need to ask Butler Chen what he thinks.” Wol ran out of Anos’s bedroom with the diary, ignoring Zhang.

Chapter Nine

July 12, 2005 5:00 a.m. George Anos’s villa, Anos’s bedroom Wol returned fifteen minutes later. “Where did you go? Luckily I trust you, or I would have called you a suspect for stealing evidence,” Zhang said unhappily to the bewildered Wol. “We were fooled by the killer—though perhaps not exactly.” Wol ignored her and spoke to himself. “What? What did you learn?” “The diary…” Wol murmured. “At first it seemed strange. Who writes narrative, criticism, and essays in the same notebook? I thought the killer had forged it. Then I asked Butler Chen. Guess what he said? This is Anos’s writing habit! Chen said Anos had kept diaries for years, but his daily life was similar and he could write only a few lines a day. So he wrote down whatever seemed worth recording, and it gradually became a free-form notebook of experiences and reflections: first what had happened recently, then valuable criticism copied from his research notebook, and finally some philosophy.
